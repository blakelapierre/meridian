The world is traced into offscreen buffers, then copied to the canvas every refresh. What is near is one ray per pixel of that picture, so bodies and the ground by your feet stay sharp at the frame rate the picture can hold. Farther than 18 m is one ray for every four pixels across and down, scaled up behind the sharp layer. The sharp rays also gather on where you are looking, rather than the middle of the camera. Eyesight carries that patch farther and wider. Focus packs more of them onto the spot. Looking raises eyesight. A target, the eyes held still, or a zoom raises focus, and focus cannot pass eyesight. The line under the picture says how far that patch stays sharp. info is pixels times the frame rate you can see times speed squared times acceleration squared. The frame target follows an inverse-square of acceleration, so it leaves 12 fps gently and only nears the display refresh under a hard acceleration. A jump is vertical speed. While the camera was still, that acceleration cuts the picture to the display refresh on the same frame instead of waiting for the next slow trace. A squared or cubed step was jumpier, and folding velocity into that curve either missed the refresh at the start of a flick or blew up at rest. With no acceleration the spare time down to 12 fps raises the resolution. The frame rate does not go below 12 while the camera is moving, including up, or while something in view is moving: a person, an animal, a shot, smoke, or a pool. Only a still camera on a still scene may go slower, and that is when the picture climbs to the native size. If a moving scene cannot hold 12 fps, the picture shrinks until the next trace can. Picture size eases toward the new target, except for that acceleration cut. The other frames stay near one picture a second until this view is cheap.
